### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Gestalt</mark> 

German word for “unified whole”.  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Gestalt principles are the “laws” that govern human perception:</mark>
- How our minds inform what we see by perceiving a series of individual elements as a whole  
- how we group elements together,  
- how we recognize patterns  
- How we simplify complex images when we perceive objects

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Gestalt and Charts</mark>

We use the principles to control grouping, attention, and interpretation when designing charts, websites, dashboards etc.  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Principles:</mark> 
- <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Proximity:</mark> Elements close to each other are perceived as a group.  
- <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Similarity:</mark> Similar elements are grouped together.  
- <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Closure:</mark> The mind fills in gaps to perceive complete shapes.  
- <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Continuity:</mark> The eye follows smooth paths or lines.  
- <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Connectedness:</mark> When visual elements are visually connected they are perceived as being grouped.  
- <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Figure-Ground:</mark> Objects are distinguished from their background.  
- <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Common Fate:</mark> Elements moving in the same direction are grouped together.

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Proximity</mark>  

Objects and shapes that are in proximity to one another appear to form groups  

The effect generated by the group is perceived as more “powerful” (more organized?) than that generated by the separate elements  

Creates the illusion of shapes, even if the various elements are not contiguous

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Similarity (invariance)</mark>  

When stimuli physically resemble one another, human brains view them as being part of the same category or object  

Stimuli that do not are viewed as part of a different object  

We can use similar characteristics to establish relationships and to encourage groupings of objects.

![[Pasted image 20260423144940.png]]

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Similarity and proximity often come together to form a visual hierarchy:</mark> 
- Either principle can dominate the other, depending on their application and combination. 
- In this visual similarity is dominating over proximity (rows over columns)

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Similarity</mark> often translates to markers of the same colour (or shape, size, and so on), being seen as representing observations from the “same” group:  

Similarity in the chart makes it easier to spot <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">similarity in the data</mark>, which reduces the overall cognitive load.  

The different colours (on the left) leave us thinking that they must mean something  

If that is not the intended interpretation, if the colours are simply used as decoration, making them all similar (as on the right) keeps the distractions to a minimum  

Similarity reduces cognitive load — but creates meaning (even if unintended)

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Focal Point</mark> 

The focal point principle states that (a few) <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">distinctive-looking objects</mark> can create focal points.  

The <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">focal point</mark> directs the reader’s eyes.  

Use sparingly - too many focal points = none

![[Pasted image 20260423145424.png]]

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Enclosures</mark> provide another way to bring out focal points  

Work best when the observations of interest are close to one another, so that the box (or curve) is clearly there but remains <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">unobtrusive</mark>.

![[Pasted image 20260423145456.png]]

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Closure</mark>

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">We see complete figures or forms even when the stimulus that reaches us is:</mark> 
- <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Incomplete</mark>  
- partially hidden by other objects  
- Or part of the information needed to make a complete picture in our minds is unavailable.  

It doesn’t mean our conclusions are correct, just that we can reach them quickly.

![[Pasted image 20260423145538.png]]

Stems from our mind’s natural tendency to <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">recognize familiar patterns</mark>, filling in any information that may be missing along the way  

The main application of closure to charts is that it removes the need for bounding boxes.

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Why?</mark> 

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Horizontal scanning is faster than vertical scanning (up to ~5×):</mark>  
- Our eyes are arranged horizontally, aligning with how we view the world  
- We process scenes more easily when they are wider than they are tall  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Key point:</mark> Design visuals to support left-to-right flow and horizontal layouts  

A. Bejan (Duke University)

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Connectedness</mark> 

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Objects connected by lines or curves are seen as part of the same group:</mark>
- Shared colour or shape can strengthen this perception
- Overlapping elements are also perceived as connected 
- These elements are seen as a single, continuous object 
- They are perceived as separate from unlinked elements  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Key point:</mark> Connectedness is a strong grouping cue that can override others (in particular proximity and similarity)

The connected line seems like the true trend  

The unconnected observations appear to be anomalies or outliers  

The data is exactly the same in both charts – just what is connected has changed

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Figure-Ground Duality</mark>

Focal point principle works well when we clearly perceive chart elements as either being figures (in focus, foreground) or as part of the (back)ground.  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Figure/Ground relationships:</mark>  
- Figure – seen as the foreground  
- Ground – seen as the background  
- Contours – “belong” to the figure  

Figure and background are not seen simultaneously

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Figure-Ground Duality</mark> 

The eye will first focus on the dogs  

When you change your focus to the ground, or seemingly negative space of the image, the eye adapts and uncovers the image of the Seattle space needle.

![[Pasted image 20260423150444.png]]

Objects in the foreground are <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">promoted</mark> in importance by the brain  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Background objects are demoted:</mark>
- Not that we don’t see them  
- May take more time before our brains get around to “reading” them.  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Can reduce the cognitive load required to parse a chart:</mark> A strong contrast between the foreground and the background makes it easier to distinguish between the two types of objects.  

Poor contrast = increased cognitive load

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Common Fate</mark>

When elements move together, humans see them as a group as we associate objects that share a common motion.  

Critical for animation and interaction

![[Pasted image 20260423150946.png]]

The law of common fate in grouping elements in the animated image is stronger here compared to the law of similarity  

Regardless of colour scheme, it is important to ensure synchronous movement so that the user can perceive this.

![[Pasted image 20260423151022.png]]

When lines or shapes come together (either in the sense of direction or location), a relationship between the underlying data observations is implied, whereas askew lines or shapes are perceived as unrelated or somewhat less related.  

We can use direction and/or movement to establish or negate relationships.  

We would not be surprised to find that groups A and B represent similar things e.g. similar product types (cakes and biscuits), while group C represents a totally different thing (e.g. tape measures)

![[Pasted image 20260423151132.png]]

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Connectedness overrides everything</mark> 

Objects connected by <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">lines or curves</mark> are perceived as related  

Sharing the same <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">colour or shape</mark> reinforces grouping  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Overlapping elements</mark> are also seen as connected  

These elements are interpreted as a <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">single, continuous object</mark>  

They are perceived as separate from <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">unlinked elements</mark>  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Key point:</mark> Connectedness is a <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">strong grouping</mark> cue that can override others

The combination of proximity with connectedness, similarity and enclosure

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Law of Prägnanz (Simplicity)</mark>  

People perceive complex images in the simplest, most organized form possible.  

It acts as an overarching principle that influences all other Gestalt principles.  

Our brain prefers order, symmetry, and simplicity over chaos and randomness.  

Our brains prefer simplicity  

Organise your charts simply and logically whenever and wherever possible.

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">You are reviewing a global development visualisation showing:</mark>  
- X-axis: GDP per capita (log scale)  
- Y-axis: Life expectancy  
- Colour: Continent  
- Size: Population  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">The goal of the visualisation is to show:</mark> “Wealthier countries tend to have higher life expectancy.”

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Proximity</mark> 

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">What works:</mark>
- Countries naturally cluster (e.g. Europe, Africa)  
- Enables quick identification of regional patterns  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Issue:</mark> Overplotting in dense regions reduces clarity  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark>  
- Supports pattern recognition  
- May increase cognitive load in dense areas

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Similarity</mark>

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">What works:</mark>  
- Colour effectively groups countries by continent  
- Reduces need for textual explanation  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Potential issue:</mark>
- Too many colours can overwhelm  
- Colour meaning must be clear (legend needed)  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark>  
- Strong grouping cue  
- Supports rapid interpretation

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Figure-Ground</mark>

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">What works:</mark>  
- Points stand out against a neutral background  
- Gridlines are subtle (if well designed)  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Issue:</mark> If gridlines are too strong → visual competition  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark>  
- Works well when contrast is balanced  
- Poor contrast increases cognitive load

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Continuity</mark> 

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">What works:</mark> 
- Overall upward trend (wealth → health)  
- Eye follows a smooth pattern across the chart  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark>  
- Supports scanning and interpretation  
- Reinforces the “big idea”

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Focal Point</mark>

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">What works:</mark> Large bubbles (e.g. China, India) attract attention  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Issue:</mark>
- May distract from overall trend  
- Users may focus on size instead of relationship  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark> Useful but must be controlled

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Connectedness (if animated)</mark>

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">What works:</mark> When animated over time, trajectories show development paths  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Issue:</mark> Without lines, relationships over time are not explicit  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark>  
- Strong when used  
- Missing in static version

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Common Fate (if animated)</mark> 

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">What works:</mark> Countries moving upward over time appear grouped  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark>  
- Reinforces regional development trends  
- Very effective in animation

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Enclosure (Not used)</mark>

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Observation:</mark> No explicit grouping boundaries  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark>  
- Acceptable — similarity already does grouping  
- Adding enclosure may clutter

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Combination of Principles</mark> 

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">What works:</mark> 
- Proximity + Similarity → strong grouping  
- Size + position → layered meaning  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Issue:</mark>  
- Too many encodings (position, size, colour)  
- Can increase cognitive load  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Evaluation:</mark>  
- Effective but complex  
- Requires careful design

### <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Guidance</mark> 

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Proximity:</mark> 
- Are related elements placed <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">close together</mark>?  
- Can I <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">remove the legend</mark> by placing labels near data?

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Similarity:</mark>
- Do colour, shape, or size <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">clearly indicate groups</mark>?  
- Is colour used <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">meaningfully</mark> (not just decoration)?  
- Are labels styled to <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">match their data</mark>?  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Closure:</mark>
- Can I <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">remove unnecessary borders</mark> or boxes?  
- Will users still perceive the structure without them?  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Connectedness:</mark>
- Are relationships shown using <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">lines or connections</mark> where appropriate?  
- Are <mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">outliers left unconnected</mark> to make them stand out?

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Continuity:</mark>  
- Is there a clear visual flow (left → right)?  
- Are elements aligned and easy to scan?  
- Can axes or lines be simplified or reduced?  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Figure–Ground:</mark>
- Do key elements stand out clearly from the background?  
- Is contrast sufficient but not overwhelming?  

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Simplicity (Prägnanz):</mark>  
- Can I remove anything unnecessary?  
- Is the chart as simple as possible while still clear?

<mark style="background: #04FF00A6;">Overall Check:</mark>
- What principle is doing the most work?  
- Are any principles conflicting or causing confusion?  
- Does the design reduce cognitive load?
